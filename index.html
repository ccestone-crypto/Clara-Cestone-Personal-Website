Temperature-Dependent Protein System

For my BIOE44 final project (with Katie Chen and Noa Oranim), I designed and validated a temperature-responsive split transcription factor that regulates gene expression. Below are excerpts from the report covering the circuit design and statistical analysis.

Engineering gene circuits that respond to precise environmental signals requires molecular switches capable of integrating multiple inputs with high specificity. Temperature is a particularly attractive physiological signal due to its tunability and orthogonality to most chemical inducers. We present a genetic circuit design employing a split transcription factor (TF) to create a temperature-dependent AND-gate system in mammalian cells. TlpA36 is a thermosensitive coiled-coil protein and mutant of the Salmonella TlpA repressor that undergoes a reversible coil-to-uncoil transition at approximately 36 °C (Naik et al., 2001). Using the TlpA36 coil regions, our TF reconstitutes a split LacI DNA-binding domain in a temperature-dependent manner, turning on downstream reporter expression when two constructs are expressed at the inducible temperature.

Our design include two protein constructs: first, the N-terminal half of LacI (residues 1-315) connected by a linker to the TlpA36 coil domain (aa 94–end) on High Risk Plasmid A (HRA); second,the C -terminal half of LacI (residues 316–360) connected by a linker the TlpA36 coil domain and a VP16 activation domain on High Risk Plasmid B (HRB). LacI is split at residue 315, a cut site shown in literature to preserve structural domains and functionality post-reassembly (Lee et al., 2024). The TlpA36 fragment is truncated to begin at residue 94, the minimal coiled-coil region identified in the literature as sufficient to maintain heterodimerization. Gly-Ser repeat linkers connect the LacI and TlpA36 domains on each plasmid, with linker length optimized using AlphaFold2 to provide sufficient spacing without disrupting domain folding. A nuclear localization signal (NLS; PKKKRKV) is included on both constructs to localize the split TF to the nucleus. The VP16 transactivation domain is placed only on Plasmid B, ensuring that transcriptional activation is contingent on reconstitution of both LacI halves.

To determine whether the observed differences in Split-LacI-TlpA36 AND-gate activation across temperatures were statistically significant, a Kruskal-Wallis H-test was performed on the percent expression values (normalized to full-length LacI-VP16 as 100% and reporter+norm as 0%). The Kruskal-Wallis test was selected as the appropriate non-parametric alternative to a one-way ANOVA, given that Shapiro-Wilk testing indicated departures from normality in several temperature groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed a statistically significant difference in AND-gate activation across the six temperatures tested (p < 0.05), indicating that temperature has a significant effect on construct activity. To identify which specific pairwise comparisons drove this result, Dunn's post-hoc test was conducted with the Benjamini-Hochberg to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Benjamini-Hochberg was selected to perform many comparisons between temperatures, detecting true positives, while still controlling the rate of false discoveries.

Following the Benjamini-Hochberg test, significant pairwise differences (adjusted p < 0.05) were observed primarily between the permissive temperatures (34 °C and 36 °C) and the more restrictive temperatures (38 °C, 38.5 °C), consistent with the expected temperature-dependent repression profile of the TlpA36 component. These findings support the conclusion that the AND gate is functionally active at 34–36 °C and is significantly repressed at temperatures of 38 °C and above, with 38.5 °C showing the lowest activation (~11%) and 34 °C showing the highest (~60%).
